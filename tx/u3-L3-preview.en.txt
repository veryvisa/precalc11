This is the brief on Unit 3 Preview, Polynomial Factoring. This toolkit is the foundation behind everything else in polynomial factoring, breaking down expressions into their simplest parts. First, always look for a greatest common factor, pulling variables at their lowest power.

Sometimes that common factor is actually a whole bracket. You can treat something like x-3 as a single letter. With four terms, try grouping them into pairs, and check your work by expanding. Second, for standard trinomials, the ax-squared-plus-bx-plus-c format,

check that leading a. When a is 1, just find numbers that multiply to c and add to b. If a isn't 1, use decomposition. Find numbers multiplying to a times c that add to b. Split the middle term and group.

Let the positive or negative signs guide your search. Third, a difference of squares is beautifully simple. Two perfect squares separated by a minus. But careful, a sum of squares literally won't factor over real numbers. You'll also see perfect square trinomials,

where the middle term is exactly twice the product of the first and last squares. Finally, factoring by substitution is your secret weapon for expressions acting like a powerful monster wearing a disguise. Just replace the repeated part with a single letter, like m. Factor the simple trinomial, substitute back, and simplify.
